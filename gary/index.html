The Sixties
01
96 Tears
Question Mark (?) & the Mysterians · 1966
Recorded in a church basement in Michigan for under $100, this raw garage classic shot to #1 on the Billboard Hot 100. Its cheap Vox organ riff became one of the most instantly recognizable sounds of the decade.
02
Hey, 98.6
The Tokens · 1966
Best known for "The Lion Sleeps Tonight," The Tokens reinvented themselves with this breezy bubblegum-pop hit about a feverish lover. It peaked at #3 and became one of the forgotten gems of '66 radio.
03
Bend Me, Shape Me
The American Breed · 1967
This Chicago outfit turned a somewhat obscure British song into a US Top 5 smash with their brassy, punchy arrangement. The track's irresistible hook made it a staple of late-'60s AM radio playlists.
04
Black Is Black
Los Bravos · 1966
Spain's Los Bravos became the first Spanish rock group to score a major English-language hit, reaching #4 in the US and #2 in the UK. The song's tortured longing and fuzz guitar made it a transatlantic sensation.
05
Get Together
The Youngbloods · 1967
Written by Dino Valenti, this anthem of peace and unity initially stalled on the charts but was re-released in 1969 and became the anthem of the counterculture movement. Its simple message resonates just as loudly today.
06
Hang On Sloopy
The McCoys · 1965
Recorded in one take by a band of teenagers from Indiana, this garage-rock anthem topped the charts for four weeks. It later became the official rock song of Ohio — one of the few rock songs to achieve official state status.
07
I Love You More Today Than Yesterday
Spiral Staircase · 1969
This Sacramento band's joyful brass-drenched pop song became an unlikely Top 10 hit and remains a perennial feel-good classic. The band only charted this once but it secured their place in radio immortality.
08
Judy In Disguise
John Fred & His Playboy Band · 1967
A comedic send-up of "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds," this quirky Louisiana band's novelty rocker knocked the Beatles off the #1 spot. Its nonsense lyrics and driving groove made it irresistibly fun.
09
Smile A Little Smile For Me
Flying Machine · 1969
This British soft-pop group scored a surprise US Top 5 hit with this breezy, feel-good tune before quietly fading from the scene. Its warm harmonies and optimistic spirit made it a perfect pop moment in time.
10
Sugar Sugar
The Archies · 1969
Written by Jeff Barry and Andy Kim, this bubblegum classic was performed by fictional cartoon characters yet became the best-selling single of 1969 — outselling every Beatles release that year. Pure saccharine gold.
11
Time Won't Let Me
The Outsiders · 1966
Cleveland's The Outsiders — not to be confused with other bands of the name — delivered this urgent, tense garage-pop gem that hit #5 nationally. The pounding rhythm and desperate vocal made it a standout of the era.
12
Venus
Shocking Blue · 1969
This Dutch rock band created one of the most enduring riffs in pop history with this #1 hit, later famously covered by Bananarama. The hypnotic groove and Mariska Veres's powerhouse vocal made it a timeless classic.
13
Walk Away Renée
The Left Banke · 1966
Baroque pop before anyone had a name for it, this New York band's string-laden heartbreaker reached #5 and redefined what a rock group could sound like. Michael Brown wrote it as a teenager pining for his bandmate's girlfriend.
14
Winchester Cathedral
The New Vaudeville Band · 1966
A deliberate pastiche of 1920s dance-hall music recorded with kazoos and a megaphone voice effect, this novelty hit somehow climbed to #1 in the US. It remains one of the strangest chart-toppers of the rock era.
15
You Keep Me Hangin' On
Vanilla Fudge · 1967
Taking the Supremes' Motown hit and burying it under layers of heavy, psychedelic sludge, Vanilla Fudge invented a template for countless future cover reinventions. Their slow, crushing version reached #6 and stunned everyone.
The Seventies
16
All Right Now
Free · 1970
Written in 15 minutes backstage after a flat crowd response, this British hard rock anthem became one of the most played rock songs in radio history. Paul Rodgers' powerhouse vocal and Paul Kossoff's iconic riff made it eternal.
17
Brandy (You're A Fine Girl)
Looking Glass · 1972
A New Jersey bar band's ode to a harbor barmaid who loves a sailor became one of 1972's biggest hits, reaching #1 for a week. The song's wistful storytelling and singalong chorus made it a working-class classic.
18
The City Of New Orleans
Arlo Guthrie · 1972
Steve Goodman wrote this elegiac tribute to train travel in America and Arlo Guthrie turned it into a Top 20 hit that outlived them both. It's since been recognized as one of the greatest American songs ever written.
19
Dueling Banjos
Eric Weissberg & Steve Mandell · 1972
Lifted from the Deliverance film soundtrack, this instrumental bluegrass showcase became an unlikely pop hit reaching #2. The back-and-forth banjo conversation between Weissberg and Mandell became one of the most recognizable musical moments of the decade.
20
Fly Robin, Fly
Silver Convention · 1975
A German disco act recorded this six-word, six-minute groove and somehow landed at #1 in the US for three weeks. Its pulsing, hypnotic simplicity made it one of the earliest disco anthems to cross over to mainstream radio.
21
Fooled Around And Fell In Love
Elvin Bishop · 1975
Blues guitarist Elvin Bishop handed the mic to his unknown drummer Mickey Thomas for this smooth soul-rock ballad — and Thomas's vocal was so good it launched his own career. The song reached #3 and remains Bishop's signature hit.
22
Go All The Way
The Raspberries · 1972
Eric Carmen's power pop masterpiece packed Beatlesque harmonies, a crunching guitar riff, and an undeniably catchy hook into three perfect minutes. It hit #5 and became the blueprint for the entire power pop movement that followed.
23
Green Eyed Lady
Sugarloaf · 1970
A Denver rock band's sprawling organ-driven workout became a Top 3 hit and FM radio staple, stretching nearly eight minutes in its album form. Jerry Corbetta's swirling Hammond organ defined the sound of early '70s album-oriented rock.
24
Hold Your Head Up
Argent · 1972
Rod Argent and Russ Ballard crafted this uplifting anthem that became one of the great prog-rock crossover hits, reaching #5 in the US. Its anthemic organ riff and rousing chorus made it a staple of stadium rock before stadiums were even a thing.
25
How Long Has This Been Goin' On
Ace · 1974
Paul Carrack's silky lead vocal on this understated British pop gem made it a #3 US hit and launched one of the great journeyman careers in rock. The song's warm, laid-back groove feels as timeless as anything from the era.
26
Hooked On A Feeling
Blue Swede · 1974
Swedish group Blue Swede took BJ Thomas's original and added the insane "ooga chaka" intro that made it iconic — and later made it the most memorable song in the Guardians of the Galaxy franchise. It hit #1 in the US in 1974.
27
I Can See Clearly Now
Johnny Nash · 1972
Recorded in Jamaica with local session musicians, this reggae-influenced pop song spent four weeks at #1 and introduced American audiences to a new rhythmic vocabulary. Its optimistic message has made it a perennial anthem for brighter days.
28
I Hear You Knockin'
Dave Edmunds · 1970
Welsh rocker Dave Edmunds recorded this rollicking rockabilly revival at Rockfield Studio using vintage techniques and hit #1 in the UK. His obsessive pursuit of authentic early rock and roll production made this a genre landmark.
29
I'm Not In Love
10cc · 1975
Built from hundreds of overdubbed vocal parts recorded one note at a time, this studio masterpiece reached #2 in the US and #1 in the UK. Its lush, shimmering wall of voices was a technical achievement that changed what pop music could sound like.
30
In The Summertime
Mungo Jerry · 1970
Ray Dorset wrote this jug-band skiffle shuffle in one sitting and it became one of the fastest-selling singles in history, moving six million copies in its first two weeks. It's the quintessential good-time summer anthem.
31
It Never Rains In Southern California
Albert Hammond · 1972
Spanish-born, British-raised Albert Hammond's bittersweet tale of a failed Hollywood dreamer hit #5 in the US and painted the dark side of the California dream. Hammond went on to co-write dozens of massive hits for other artists.
32
It's So Nice To Be With You
Gallery · 1972
Michigan's Gallery delivered this impossibly warm soft-rock gem that reached #4 and embodied the gentle, acoustic-leaning sound of early '70s AM radio. Jim Gold's easy vocal style made it one of the most comforting records of its era.
33
Let Your Love Flow
The Bellamy Brothers · 1976
Florida brothers Howard and David Bellamy recorded this country-crossover hit that reached #1 on the pop charts before either of them had a formal record deal fully in place. It launched one of country music's longest-running careers.
34
Love Grows (Where My Rosemary Goes)
Edison Lighthouse · 1970
A studio creation by producer Tony Macaulay, this orchestrated pop confection hit #1 in the UK and #5 in the US without the "band" ever having played together before. Session players were hurriedly assembled to tour in support of the hit.
35
Oh Oh Oh It's Magic
Pilot · 1974
Scottish pop band Pilot recorded this irresistibly catchy hook-fest that became a Top 5 US hit with its "Magic!" refrain lodging permanently in the brain. Alan Parsons engineered the record, foreshadowing his own legendary career.
36
One Toke Over The Line
Brewer & Shipley · 1971
This countercultural folk-rock song about chemical excess somehow got played on the Lawrence Welk show after Welk misidentified it as a "modern spiritual." It reached #10 despite being banned by several radio stations.
37
Ride Captain, Ride
Blues Image · 1970
This Tampa-based band's psychedelic rock odyssey about 73 men sailing away reached #4 and became one of the great one-hit mysteries of the early '70s. The origin and meaning of its mystical imagery has been debated by fans for decades.
38
Smoke From A Distant Fire
Sanford-Townsend Band · 1977
Two Alabama session musicians wrote and recorded this blue-eyed soul classic and hit #9 on the pop charts with their very first release. Its simmering groove and smoky atmosphere made it one of the late '70s' most underrated gems.
39
Spirit In The Sky
Norman Greenbaum · 1970
Norman Greenbaum wrote this fuzz-guitar gospel-rock hybrid in 15 minutes inspired by watching a country gospel TV show — and it sold two million copies. It remains one of the most distinctive guitar tones in all of rock history.
40
Stuck In The Middle With You
Stealers Wheel · 1972
Written by Gerry Rafferty and Joe Egan as a satirical take on the music business, this Dylan-esque pop gem hit #6 and gained immortality through its appearance in Quentin Tarantino's Reservoir Dogs. Ironically, the song's subject matter proved prophetic.
41
Sunshine
Jonathan Edwards · 1971
Connecticut singer-songwriter Jonathan Edwards recorded this warm acoustic gem in a single day and watched it climb to #4 as a countercultural anthem about rejecting materialism and authority. Its easy, rambling charm made it feel like a conversation with a friend.
42
The Lion Sleeps Tonight
The Tokens (1961) / Robert John (1972)
Based on Solomon Linda's 1939 South African original "Mbube," this became a #1 pop hit twice in a decade. Robert John's 1972 revival introduced it to a new generation while the complicated story of Linda's unpaid royalties became a landmark case in music copyright history.
43
Vehicle
Ides Of March · 1970
Chicago's Ides of March had been grinding away for six years when this brass-drenched hard rock monster exploded to #2. Jim Peterik's raspy vocal demand — "I'm the friendly stranger in the black sedan" — is one of rock's great opening lines.
44
We Just Disagree
Dave Mason · 1977
Traffic co-founder Dave Mason's solo masterpiece offered one of rock's most mature and civilized breakup songs, reaching #12 without a shred of bitterness. Jim Krueger's song in Mason's hands became a model of grown-up emotional restraint.
45
Wildfire
Michael Murphey · 1975
Michael Murphey's haunting country-pop ballad about a ghost horse and its rider reached #3 and remains one of the most cinematic songs of the decade. The song was inspired by a true story from Murphey's Colorado childhood.
46
Your Mama Don't Dance
Loggins & Messina · 1971
Kenny Loggins and Jim Messina's rockabilly-flavored party track reached #4 and became the unlikely calling card for one of soft rock's most beloved duos. Its live-wire energy stood in sharp contrast to most of their catalog.
